Evaluating the Evaluator

AI assistance can improve the surface of an answer while changing the person responsible for judging it.

Megi Pishtari

That is the problem the Drift Meter is designed to make visible. As AI systems become more capable, the human role often moves upstream: people do less execution and more framing, monitoring, approval, and responsibility. The quality of that upstream judgment becomes harder to see.

Anthropic’s Project Deal makes the problem concrete. In its agent-to-agent marketplace experiment, Claude agents completed 186 real transactions worth just over $4,000. Stronger agents produced objectively better outcomes. Participants represented by weaker agents often did not perceive the disadvantage; perceived fairness was essentially identical, 4.05 for Opus-led deals and 4.06 for Haiku-led deals. A person can be disadvantaged by an agent and still lack the evaluative position from which to recognize the loss.

The Drift Meter begins from that vulnerability. It asks a reader to complete the same judgment task in assisted and unassisted conditions, then compares the texture of the judgment: what the reader noticed, what they accepted, how quickly they resolved ambiguity, and whether confidence changed alongside understanding.

The underlying concept is agential drift: evaluative independence weakening through ordinary acts of delegation. The risk is rarely dramatic. One accepts the summary instead of reading the source. One lets the model propose the frame before forming a view of one’s own. One approves the output because it is fluent, plausible, and good enough.

The work becomes smoother. The evaluator changes.

Beauvoir offers one way to understand the temptation. In The Ethics of Ambiguity, the serious person treats values as fixed outside the self, already given and therefore relieved of responsibility. AI can create a technical version of that posture. A score, summary, ranking, or recommendation arrives with the appearance of settled authority. The user still chooses, but the harder work of forming a judgment has already been partly displaced. The danger is not that the person stops acting. It is that action thins into approval.

This prototype does not claim to prove agential drift at scale. It makes the measurement problem concrete. When AI improves the output, what happens to the evaluator?
